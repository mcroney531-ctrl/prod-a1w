What I Did
  • Attempted direct teammate lookups/calls in the chat session: arguably the most critical feature on the entire app. It was the first thing I asked about when I logged in, and something I kept testing throughout the week. There's a big difference between seeing a continuum, a graph, or a side-by-side comparison of you and a colleague, and having that data fully loaded and referenceable in essentially infinite contexts — being able to flat-out state, without sugarcoating and without fear of judgment, "I am facing this problem with this colleague," and actually talk or work through it. That's significant, and it was a point of emphasis for me early and often. It was admittedly a bit bumpy at times — largely because it depends on the other colleague being fully activated — and it got noticeably better over the week as more people came online. Something I'll be watching closely, and hoping to see working seamlessly soon. More detail on this will be documented separately in the UX/UI survey.
  • Inquired about the tool's own capabilities and discussed potential use cases with it conversationally: I personally think discussing what a large language model can and can't do, and how it interacts with you, is — or should be — standard practice, and a quick way to align and set expectations. I thought the tool was pretty clear in its communication on this front.
  • Attempted to launch a "project": not always, but I tend to give LLM chat sessions specific purposes — general talk, article drafting, a specific task — and wanted to do the same here: establish a "purpose," a longer-term environment with context set upfront. A common mistake with LLMs is skipping that step entirely, or stopping at the cheesy "you are a world-class [X]" framing instead of plainly stating why you're there and what you're doing. Establishing those parameters gives a session far more purpose and precise direction — and a long-term, purposeful setup like this isn't just practical for the user, it inherently encourages them to return. This is something I want to keep evaluating over time: the project I started is for a real, ongoing piece of work I'll run in parallel with my actual job to see how it holds up.
  • Sought guidance on rewording a (mock) harsh message draft: personally, I think this is one of the cleaner use cases — one that can cut through effectively to a wide range of audiences. We all have things we hold in or don't fully address head-on: maybe there's no proper outlet, maybe we don't want to seem dumb, maybe we're not comfortable naming how a certain behavior is really bothering us, or maybe we just don't feel confident in how we've worded something important. Or the kneejerk instinct is to say it a certain way, when saying it like that would only cause more problems or unintended consequences. This was something I really wanted to try, and I'll keep testing it in real settings — I think there's wide "human" appeal here.

    Doing this also helped me think again in augmentation terms. The results I got back were good, but I rarely take a first draft from AI as final — the way I tend to work with it is to take the output and fine-tune/iterate until I'm happy with the message, or whatever we're putting together. So the natural next thought was: what if I included examples of messages I've actually sent, to infuse my own voice into these draft outputs — along with anything else that could make the output richer or more useful? At that point, you and the coach are operating on a more similar playing field. On a larger scale, you could even turn it on yourself and ask it to "evaluate my messaging style" — and since your data and tendencies are already infused, that gets factored into the eval too. The more you lean into the augmentation, the richer and more valuable the tool starts to feel.
  • Light stress-testing: pushed toward its boundaries — not stress-tested on purpose, just asked in passing — the tool held those too. Asked about technical implementation while troubleshooting, it appropriately declined to explain internals. Asked about pasting a colleague's chat exchange in for help, the response was "yes, but" — with a clear caveat to redact sensitive details. Not a blanket refusal, not reckless — good judgment, unprompted, in a moment that wasn't a test.

Early Impressions

  • Top-line, one week in: overall impression is genuinely positive. Real potential is already visible, and use cases are surfacing independently rather than needing to be prompted.
  • This can genuinely be an everyday app for many people. Where the original product serves more of a "touch-consultation" purpose, this one's dynamic component makes it exponentially more versatile.
  • More versatile than anticipated — which drives returnability. It can help with things in the moment, big or small, but catered with real specificity. Where [OLD-PROD] informed you of preferences and tendencies broadly — how they might affect a situation in the abstract — this infuses that same knowledge into real-time, specific moments on demand, catered to the user. That's what increases how often someone actually opens the app, exponentially.
  • This is already happening — and that's a good thing. This ties back to an important concept I mentioned in the AI Arc document, which was that the strongest AI successes and the most transferable use cases are the ones people are already doing, just unstructured — on a personal ChatGPT or Claude account, or their work Copilot account, consulting about something, rewording a message, whatever it is. What's different here isn't the behavior, it's the structure: this product comes with your assessment data loaded in, alongside your colleagues' assessment data, giving it context an ad hoc tool never has. The pitch isn't convincing people to start doing something new — it's pointing to people already doing it and giving that behavior a properly contextualized home.
  • There's real value in comparing and contrasting [OLD-PROD] and [NEW-PROD]. It goes back to the "what are you already doing — and already doing well" framing that lends itself to successful AI adoption. Thinking about this tool with a tether to familiar terms helped clarify my thinking, but it also made the contrasts click clearly: "oh, X can be an extension of Y" or "wow, this could make the experience behind Z feel so much richer now." Instead of an abstract "AI" or "AI-powered" pitch, it becomes a plain what-is vs. what-can-be.
  • "What if you could talk to [OLD-PROD]?" The words that kept coming up all week were static and dynamic — that's the core differentiator, illustrated further in the Scenario below. Framing it as "talking to [OLD-PROD]" makes it feel more familiar to people less comfortable with AI, because the key point isn't anything sci-fi — it's simply that [OLD-PROD] is AI-powered underneath. This product, and its dynamic layer, extends what already made [OLD-PROD] great; it doesn't replace it. Prediction: down the line, this could develop into an "[OLD-PROD] Classic" vs. "[OLD-PROD]" kind of split.
  • The harness holds — even off-script. None of this week's strongest evidence came from a planned test. It came from just talking to the product about something else entirely — and the harness held anyway.
  • The Augmentation Mindset Shift: while troubleshooting a small issue, it became clear this tool can be used in parallel with work — not just consulted the way a static resource is consulted. Most tools in this category are reference material: here is information, here are tips, here is data, go use it. This one, used well, behaves differently — closer to augmentation than reference, worked with in real time rather than looked up and left. The notable part isn't that the tool can do this — it's that no one was following an anticipated workflow to get there. The conversation surfaced it unprompted.
💡The Key Unlock

This is the floor, not the ceiling.

Out of the box, every user gets the same baseline: the AI coach, the standard features, a reasonable starting experience. What happens after that point is not fixed — the ceiling is set by what the user brings to it and how far they choose to take it. Nearly everything below is either evidence of that floor, or a response to how high the ceiling could go.

Supporting Evidence — The Actual Exchange

Me: "I think there's potential — with limits — to use you nicely in the AI-augmented workflow age. Where yes, we discuss how to talk about stuff, but also kinda working 'side-by-side' on things too, if you will. My goal is to think beyond the basics and show my team how, in essence, your program and the features on this app are the floor, but the dynamic nature of AI comms means that it's NOT the ceiling in the same way static content is — in other words, beyond 'what you see is what you get.' I know you can't give internal technical details, but even still, we did some light tech troubleshooting. That might not be a use case, but it illustrates that you can go where the user takes you, within reason."

Product: "Yes, that's the stronger product story: the app's visible features are the floor, while the conversation becomes a flexible layer around them. A user can move from 'help me understand this dynamic' to 'rewrite this message,' 'compare these names,' 'spot the pattern,' or 'walk through what I should test next,' as long as the task stays within the system's boundaries."

"The key distinction is that the AI isn't just delivering content. It's helping the user think, inspect, create, and act alongside the product. For you, that matters because your instinct to preserve harmony can sometimes keep you in analysis; a side-by-side partner can help turn reflection into a message, test, or next move. The limits are part of the promise, not a weakness: it should be clear about what it can't access, what it's inferring, and when the user needs to verify something in the app or with a person. That transparency makes the 'beyond the interface' experience feel useful rather than magical."

Wishlist

A few rough edges surfaced this week. Framed under the core thesis, these read less as broken promises and more as specific spots where the floor is still settling.

  • Memory across sessions — stated preferences don't appear to persist. A current limit on how high the floor sits today, not evidence the ceiling isn't real.
  • Ability to rename conversations. This goes back to giving a chat a purpose — it's not a one-off conversation, it's the establishment of an environment, and that invites returnability and consistent use.
  • Let the daily tip ask, not assume — right now it assumes a generic course-participant context rather than adjusting to the actual role, a sag in one specific spot (not a signal the whole product is unreliable, and one the product team has separately flagged as still in progress). Rather than assuming role and context upfront, a light clarifying question on first use could invite the user to self-customize — self-correcting through interaction instead of requiring a manual rebuild later.

    The daily tip, particularly since it arrives as a Teams ping, feels like an invitation into the full app — which makes it even more critical to get right. If it comes off inaccurate, unhelpful, or stale, it signals that the rest of the experience will be similar. Rather than trying to engineer the "right" context for everyone — which is borderline impossible — making it more conversational and inquiry-based serves as a better invitation, and takes some of that precision pressure off. Worth noting too: responding in Teams automatically opens a conversation on the web-app side with that exchange. This is an important one to get right, and something I've heard mentioned in almost every meeting that's discussed the product since this initial-access mini rollout. Asking and arriving at the daily tip/focus together, based on inquiry, makes it feel more collaborative in nature — and encourages the kind of augmented workflow that will make this product a successful one.
  • "Quick actions" — this is really just an extension of a mechanism already on the app: the "More on relationships," "More on conflict," etc. buttons that launch a coach conversation with a preloaded prompt based on which one you clicked. Those are more topic/concept-based; this would be more action-based. Example: Quick actions → "Help me craft a message" → launches a coach conversation with "Great, what is the message, and who are we sending it to?" If research on use cases has already been done, this could be a great way to put that data to use. The tool is extremely vast and can be used in a myriad of ways, which is great but can also be overwhelming. Quick actions could be a fast, practical way for even a light user to say "well, I do use it sometimes, with the help-me-craft-a-message feature" — straightforward, but still carrying all the utility of the tool. It also helps shift the mindset from "this can be informative" to "I can actually do things in here."
  • Find ways to pair the [WSM] experience with the tool. [WSM] already tends to be seen as an interactive module — putting AI power behind that seems like it could pair beautifully. The next step, if possible: use that module's data informatively elsewhere too. Example — in an action plan, you pick a colleague you want to work better with and type in specifics to develop your plan for that module's focus area. Imagine that data informing your daily tip whenever that colleague is on the same call or project as you.

    The same logic applies to the "Get Advice" feature. In theory, that's more or less the larger point of the site already — but there's something simple and approachable about the basic Get Advice flow: you select a colleague you want advice on working with, plus what specifically you want advice on. That could be made even more interactive, dynamic, and accessible for the average user, precisely because it's already targeted and specific — and familiar to anyone who's used [OLD-PROD]. More of the "what are you already doing well" concept.

What This Means for How We Teach It

The traditional model for rolling out a feature is straightforward: the feature is static, a walkthrough explains what it does, and that's sufficient. This product doesn't fit that model cleanly. Its actual ceiling depends entirely on what the user brings to it — a one-time walkthrough won't teach someone how to get long-term value out of something that rewards ongoing use.

The practical implication: learning content likely needs to shift from here's what this feature does to here's how you can make the most of it — teaching usage patterns and habits, not just surface functionality. You only get out what you put in, and that has to be taught deliberately. It won't be assumed correctly on its own.

Questions Moving Forward

  • What are the "early wins" a user can experience that build belief in a product and a desire to return to it for genuine guidance?
  • What research was done during development on how people actually use AI at work, and what did it reveal? That's the kind of insight that could surface clear-cut use cases worth showcasing and advertising.
  • Have we considered role-specific use cases? A project manager constantly checking in with reports on their progress or giving them direction is likely to use this quite differently than an individual contributor. This loops back to the research question above, and connects to the "early wins" a user can build on and want to keep returning to.
  • What cultural and social tone should we actively encourage around this product? [OLD-PROD] took real, deliberate effort to build its own culture and language — the shift from "I've done [OLD-PROD]" to "we use [OLD-PROD]." Something I've been personally wondering about AI more broadly: when does it become normal to talk openly about a genuine revelation from a conversation with an AI, without it feeling stigmatized? Will people share their wins with this product openly, or does it feel more private by nature — and what's our role in shaping that language?
  • Like the useful "here's how you can use this to help yourself advance, improve, and progress" framing from the UMD course — what are the ways we ensure we're presenting this tool in that same light, rather than leaning too heavily on novelty, or a "look what it can do" pitch?
  • What does long-term, consistent QA look like for a product this dynamic and responsive, in an environment where things change quickly and human behavior is unpredictable? Today's novelty can become tomorrow's roadblock, hallucination, or stale feature — how do we ensure consistently high-quality outputs over time?
  • Bonus: not really a question so much as an anticipated one. With our current assessments and products, we already get asked whether they hold up and translate across cultures. AI is known to carry bias — it's essentially impossible to have a fully unbiased LLM, since it's trained on human data and humans hold biases too, so even the cleanest systems still have those loops. I'd anticipate eventually fielding similar questions about cultural crossover, hold-up, and translation with this product's AI layer — good to start thinking about now, ahead of being asked.
